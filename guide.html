Guide to the Alenuihāhā Channel briefing

This briefing gathers what is known about the wind in the Alenuihāhā Channel, the 25 nm gap between Maui and the Big Island, into one page built twice a day (about 06:30 and 18:30 HST). It puts the official NWS forecasts next to several weather models, the observations that show what is actually happening, and an analysis of what drives the channel wind, and then checks the forecasts against satellite measurements.

It is not an official forecast. It is meant for study, learning and awareness. Always read the NWS Honolulu forecasts and warnings, check each product's issue and valid time, and make your own decision.

What every part of the briefing shows, where its data comes from, how it is made, what it assumes and where it can mislead, with notes on the weather behind it. Built with the briefing of Sat 03 Oct 2026 06:30 HST.

A reading order for a crossing

  1. Channel outlook: the headline answer: what NWS and the models expect mid-channel and at the departure, and how sure they are.
  2. Sources: are the inputs current? A red banner means a critical forecast is out of date.
  3. Local zones: the official PHZ121 (Alenuihāhā Channel) and PHZ123 (Leeward Big Island) forecasts, word for word.
  4. Model comparison: where the models agree or not, hour by hour, for each part of the channel.
  5. Wind maps and REFS maps: where in the channel the strong wind sits, and how uncertain it is.
  6. Channel analysis: why the wind is doing what it is (the high, the pressure gradients, the inversion).
  7. Verification: how well each source has actually done lately, so you know whom to trust.

Times, units and colours

  • Time is Hawaiʻi Standard Time (HST, UTC−10, no daylight saving), usually with UTC (“Z”) beside it. The ASCAT image composite is labelled in UTC. “N h ago” ages are recomputed from your device's clock every minute.
  • Wind is in knots (kt) everywhere; model output in m/s is converted (×1.944). Pressure is in hPa (= mb). Distances on the page are nautical miles (1 nm = 1.852 km); heights are metres, with feet on the sounding charts.
  • Directions are where the wind comes from (true); arrows on charts point downwind. The channel axis runs 066°/246°: the trades blow down it from the northeast toward the southwest exit.
  • Status colours: green = current; amber = late or partly available; red = stale, expired, missing or a warning; grey = live or undated. Headline chips: red warning, amber watch or advisory, blue statement.
  • Wind-speed colours on the maps follow the University of Washington WRF palette in 5 kt bands (“25” means 22.5–27.5 kt). It is not a smooth ramp: it turns back toward cool colours above 30 kt, so read the legend.
  • Dark mode follows your device until you pick one with the button. The page's own charts are colour-inverted in dark mode; images whose colours carry meaning (satellite, radar, charts, model maps) are only dimmed.
  • Refresh imagery buttons reload the live NOAA images without rebuilding the page.

The channel

Why the channel is windy

The Hawaiian Islands sit in the trade winds: the steady northeasterly flow on the southern side of the North Pacific (subtropical) High, a large area of high pressure usually centred well north-northeast of the islands. Air flows out of the high and, turned by the Earth's rotation, arrives at Hawaiʻi from the east-northeast.

The islands are tall: Haleakalā on Maui rises to 3,055 m (10,023 ft); Kohala, Mauna Kea and Mauna Loa on the Big Island are higher still. The trade-wind layer is usually only 1.5–2.5 km deep, capped by the trade-wind inversion (below). Air that meets the mountains cannot go over them easily, so it piles up on the windward side, where pressure rises a little, and goes around them. The Alenuihāhā Channel is the gap between two of the tallest obstacles, so the flow is squeezed through it: pressure is higher at the windward entrance than in the lee at the exit, and the air speeds up down that pressure drop. The result is a gap jet, often 10–15 kt stronger than the open-ocean trades, strongest near the narrowest point (the throat, from Upolu Point to the Kaupō–Kīpahulu coast) and fanning out downstream along Maui's south coast.

The ingredients the briefing watches

  • The Pacific High: its strength and position set how hard the trades blow. A strong high to the north-northeast, lined up with the channel, drives the strongest trades.
  • The pressure gradient across the islands (buoys 51000 to the northeast and 51002 to the southwest): the large-scale push.
  • The pressure gradient across the channel itself (shore stations on both sides): the local push that actually drives the jet.
  • The wind feeding in and out, and whether it is lined up with the channel axis: flow aimed straight down the channel funnels through it more readily.
  • The season: Hawaiʻi has two. In kau (May–October) the Pacific High is strong and far north, the trades are steady and the inversion persistent: the channel's windiest, most regular months. In hoʻoilo (November–April) the high weakens and shifts south, and fronts, upper-level troughs and kona lows interrupt the trades, sometimes reversing them. The verification page groups passes by these two seasons and by the meteorological quarters, which separate the spring and autumn transitions.
  • The trade-wind inversion: a lid on the trade-wind layer. A strong, low lid confines the flow to a thin layer that must squeeze through the gap, which speeds it up; a weak or missing one lets it spread upward and weaken.

What it shows

Every chart, map and score uses the same areas, defined once in config/channel_geometry.yaml, so that a meteogram, a map and a verification score can never disagree about where an area ends.

How it's made

The briefing, section by section

Channel outlook

What it shows

The crossing decision at a glance: any NWS headlines, the next three PHZ121 forecast periods, the models' median wind mid-channel now and at its highest over the next 24 and 48 h, the highest off Honokōhau, and how much the models agree. Every figure links to the section it comes from, so the card never becomes a second source of truth.

Data and timing

The PHZ121 and PHZ123 zone forecasts, and the model winds (RRFS, NBM, UH WRF).

How it's made
  • Headlines: every headline in the two zone forecasts, one chip each: red for warnings, amber for watches and advisories, blue for statements.
  • NWS: the three periods starting with the one in force now, as lo–hi kt, gusts and direction, parsed from the text (see zone forecasts).
  • Models (median): at each hour, the median of RRFS (the extended run), NBM and UH WRF over the area's average wind, when at least two answer. “Highest next 24 h/48 h” is the largest value of that median series, with its time.
  • Model agreement: how far the last RRFS runs spread (“Runs agree” within 5 kt, “disagree” above 10 kt), the chance of 30 kt+ mid-channel from the RRFS ensemble members, and the mean REFS spread over 48 h (see model comparison).
How to read it

Start here, then follow the links for anything that surprises you.

Assumptions and limits. The median is smoothed across models, so its peak is lower than any single model's peak on a gusty or uncertain day. The NWS grid is not in this median (it is in the model-comparison band).

Sun and moon, and the sky line

What it shows

Sunrise, sunset, twilight, local apparent noon and the sun's height then, the moon's phase and rise/set, a spring/neap tide hint and the UV forecast, for mid-channel (the throat) with the Honokōhau difference.

Data and timing

Computed on the page's build machine, no download, except the UV Index (US EPA forecast for ZIP 96755, Kapaʻau).

How it's made
  • Sunrise and sunset: the US Naval Observatory's “Almanac for Computers” method, sun's centre 90.833° from the zenith (refraction and the sun's radius). Civil twilight is the sun 6° down, nautical 12°.
  • Local apparent noon: 12:00 minus longitude and the equation of time (NOAA's series). The channel is 6° west of the 150°W meridian HST keeps time by, so LAN averages about 24 minutes after clock noon (12:07 in early November to 12:38 in mid February). Noon altitude = 90° − |latitude − declination|.
  • Moon: the Astronomical Almanac's low-precision series; a principal phase is named only within about a day of it. Moonrise and set are for the upper limb on a sea horizon.
  • Tides: “spring” from a day before to three days after new or full moon, “neap” around the quarters. A hint only: Hawaiʻi's tides are small (about 2 ft at Kawaihae).
  • UV: the EPA's hourly forecast, in WHO bands (low, moderate, high, very high, extreme) with rough burn times.
Assumptions and limits. Times are good to about a minute (sun) or a few minutes (moon). The UV forecast is for North Kohala, not the water, where reflection adds to it.
Learn. Rule 20 of the collision regulations requires navigation lights from sunset to sunrise, not from dark. Civil twilight (~22 minutes here: short near the tropics) is the practical limit for coming in; by nautical dusk the horizon is gone. LAN is the time of a noon sight.

The forecast check strip and ASCAT card

What it shows

One line near the top: how each forecast did against the ASCAT satellite over the last 7 days, forecasts made up to a day ahead. In the ASCAT section, a card with the newest pass and what was forecast for it.

How it's made

Bias is the forecast minus ASCAT, averaged over the passes; “about right” means within 1 kt. For the NWS zone forecast, “right” means the middle of what ASCAT saw fell inside the forecast range. See Verification for everything behind it.

Sources: issue, valid and expiry times

What it shows

Every input of this build, with its time, age, expiry and a status: is it current, late, stale or missing? A red banner appears if the PHZ121 channel forecast is expired, stale or missing.

How it's made

Each row's time is labelled for what it means: Issued (a forecast), Valid (an analysis), Observed, Run (a model run's start), Updated (the NWS grid) or Live (images that load from NOAA when you open the page). A product that states its own expiry is judged by it. Otherwise by its normal cadence: current if its age is within one cadence plus a margin (usually 6 h), “late: newer issue overdue” within two cadences, stale after that. Cadences: High Seas 6 h; discussions, offshore, coastal and zone forecasts 12 h; RRFS extended 6 h, latest 3 h; NBM 1 h; REFS 6 h; UH WRF 24 h (+36 h, since PacIOOS posts it about a day late); buoys and stations 1 h; Hilo sounding 12 h.

How to read it

Glance at the counts line; open the table when something is amber or red. A stale model row means that model's lines on the comparison are old.

Pacific charts (OPC chart matrix)

What it shows

The Ocean Prediction Center's surface analysis and forecasts, 500 mb and wind/wave charts for now and 24–96 h ahead: the Pacific-scale pattern of highs, fronts, upper ridges and troughs, and sea state.

Data and timing

ocean.weather.gov. Surface analysis every 6 h, 500 mb every 12 h, wind/wave once a day (00Z); forecasts from the 00Z and 12Z runs.

How it's made

OPC's fixed “latest” image addresses can keep serving frozen charts (in September 2026 the 72 and 96 h surface forecasts had been suspended for weeks while their fixed addresses still showed a 2 September chart). So each chart is found in OPC's dated archive for the last 10 days instead; a chart OPC is not issuing shows “Not currently issued”, never an old one. Forecasts in one row come from one model run where OPC has posted them. The printed VALID and ISSUED times are read off each chart by text recognition; if the chart disagrees with the time expected, the chart wins and says so, and an unreadable stamp is shown as “computed”. Each chart is current if within its cadence plus 6 h, late within two cadences, stale after (dimmed).

Assumptions and limits. Text recognition can misread a stamp; the cell then says “computed”. OPC posts charts 1.5–5 h after their nominal time.
Learn. The surface analysis shows the Pacific High's centre and pressure, and fronts approaching from the northwest in winter. The 500 mb chart shows the upper-level pattern that steers surface systems: a ridge overhead supports a strong, stable high; an upper trough nearby can weaken the trades and the inversion. The wind/wave chart shows sea state across the basin.

Satellite imagery (GOES-West)

What it shows

GOES-18 GeoColor loops for the tropical Pacific and the Hawaiʻi sector: true colour by day, infrared by night.

Data and timing

Loaded live from NOAA/NESDIS STAR each time the page opens.

Learn. Trade-wind days show rows of small cumulus streaming from the east-northeast, clearing in the lee of the big islands. Local marine zone forecasts

What it shows

The official NWS forecasts for the zones around the channel: PHZ121 Alenuihāhā Channel and PHZ123 Big Island Leeward Waters open, PHZ117, 118 and 122 folded.

Data and timing

tgftp.nws.noaa.gov, about every 12 h and when amended.

How it's made

Bulletin cards (all NWS text products): the issue time from the product's issuance line; the expiry from its “Expires” line or its zone code line (e.g. PHZ121-251115- = expires the 25th at 11:15Z); headlines as chips; sections as titled blocks; the original text always one tap away.

Reading the numbers (for the outlook, the model chart and verification): periods are placed in time (days 06–18 HST, nights 18–06, “rest of today” to 18:00); “25 to 30 knots” is 25–30; “to 30” or “up to 30” is 25–30; “less than 10” is 0–10; “around 15” is 13–17; changes such as “rising to 30 kt after midnight” split a period.

Assumptions and limits. A zone forecast gives one range for the whole zone, which also has to cover its windiest corner, so it reads high for much of the zone on a jet day.
Learn. A Small Craft Advisory in Hawaiʻi is issued for winds of 25–33 kt and/or seas of 10 ft or more; a Gale Warning for 34–47 kt.

What it shows

The forecast 10 m wind for each channel area, from every model the briefing reads, on one chart, 3 h back to 72 h ahead.

How it's made
  • Area values: every model grid point inside the area (water only): mean of the point speeds; gust = the 90th percentile of the gust field across the area (the gustier part, not one point); direction from the averaged wind vector.
  • The band is the range (lowest to highest) across the models and the NWS grid at each hour, the grey line their median, when at least two answer. The black line is NWS. Short gaps (≤3 h) are bridged.
  • Direction arrows under the chart: the models' average direction, weighted by speed, pointing downwind.
  • RRFS run-to-run: over the next 18 h, the worst hour's spread of the last RRFS runs' area means. Runs agree ≤5 kt, some spread 5–10 kt, disagree >10 kt.
  • Chance of 30 kt+: from the RRFS ensemble members (below). Red dashed lines mark 25 kt (Small Craft) and 34 kt (gale).
  • On the zone tabs, the NWS zone forecast is drawn as a box per period (its range), a dotted gust line and a headline strip.
How to read it

A narrow band and settled runs: a confident forecast. A wide band, or runs that keep shifting: plan for the high side.

Assumptions and limits. The models' grids (2.5–6 km) are coarse next to the channel's coastal detail: the Kohala coast and the Maui lee are where they differ most. UH WRF's run can be a day or more old (check Sources). NBM, being a blend, is smooth and tends to run low in the jet (see Verification).
Learn. Deterministic models give one answer; an ensemble runs the model many times with small differences to show the range of possibilities. A calibrated blend (NBM) corrects and combines many models. Watching successive runs (run-to-run consistency) is a quick way to judge confidence.

REFS ensemble by area

What it shows

The RRFS ensemble for each area: the ensemble-mean wind with a ±1 spread band, and the chance of 20, 25, 30, 34, 35 or 40 kt.

Data and timing

REFS products from NOMADS (00/06/12/18Z, hourly to 60 h) and the five RRFS ensemble members.

How it's made
  • Spread is the ensemble's standard deviation: roughly two members in three fall inside the band.
  • Two ways to ask “how likely”, and they are not two opinions: Area average counts, each hour, how many of 11 answers (the five members of the latest run and of the run 6 h before, plus the deterministic RRFS) put the area's average wind at or above 25, 30 or 34 kt. Within ~20 nm (REFS) is NOAA's product: the chance of the threshold anywhere within roughly 20 nm of a point, so an area beside the jet can show a high chance while its own average stays light.
How to read it

For whether the water you will be on reaches a threshold, the area average is the more direct answer.

Assumptions and limits. REFS's mean wind runs above the individual RRFS winds (about 1 kt mid-channel, up to ~5 kt along the Kohala coast where directions vary): read it as the upper side of typical. Eleven answers make a coarse probability (steps of ~9%).
Learn. Neighbourhood probability answers “could it happen near here”; area probability answers “will it happen here”. Using the previous run too (time-lagging) is a cheap way to widen an ensemble.

ASCAT satellite winds

What it shows

The latest coastal ASCAT wind images over the islands (up to 12 passes from the last 3 days) and, above them, the forecast check for the newest pass.

Data and timing

NOAA/NESDIS STAR's coastal ultra-high-resolution images, from EUMETSAT's MetOp-B and MetOp-C.

Assumptions and limits. Times on the images are UTC. Not every pass covers the channel.
Learn. ASCAT is a radar scatterometer: it measures how rough the sea surface is and turns that into the 10 m wind, in 12.5 km cells, about twice a day here (around 09–10 and 20–22 HST). It is the only measurement that sees the whole channel at once, which is why the briefing verifies against it.

Channel analysis: the Pacific High

What it shows

The high-pressure centres (“lobes”) of the North Pacific High now, 24 h ago and in 24 and 48 h, ranked by how hard each pushes toward the channel.

Data and timing

GFS sea-level pressure (0.25°, 1° fallback) through NOMADS, every 6 h.

How it's made
  • Candidate centres: connected areas within 12 hPa of the domain's highest pressure (25–50N, 125–180W). Each lobe's size is the closed isobar around its centre, 1–16 hPa below it, sized to between 0.6 and 12 million km² (the red outline). Island-sized artefacts and edge maxima are rejected.
  • Gradient toward the channel = (centre pressure − pressure at the channel) ÷ distance, per 1000 nm.
  • Alignment: the angle between the direction from the high to the channel and the channel axis.
  • Push along the axis: the geostrophic wind that gradient implies (air density 1.225 kg/m³, Coriolis at the channel's latitude), resolved along the axis. Lobes are ranked by gradient × alignment.
Assumptions and limits. The gradient is a straight-line average over 1,000–2,000 nm, not the local gradient, and “push along the axis” is an index of forcing, not a wind you will see.
Learn. A high well to the north-northeast, strong and lined up with the channel, sets up strong trades; a high that weakens, retreats or is displaced by a front weakens them.

Channel analysis: local isobar field

What it shows

The GFS pressure field over a ~180 nm box around the channel, and the geostrophic wind it implies.

How it's made

A flat plane is fitted to the GFS sea-level pressure over water within ±1.5° of the throat (least squares). Its slope gives the pressure gradient; the geostrophic wind follows from it (air density 1.225 kg/m³, Coriolis at the channel's latitude), with its direction, its angle to the axis and the part along it. The fit's quality (R², points, ocean share, stability when points are dropped) is graded High, Moderate or Low.

Assumptions and limits. One value for the whole box: differences between entrance, throat and exit come from the high-resolution models. Geostrophic wind ignores friction and the terrain's squeeze, so the real channel wind can be well above or below it.
Learn. Geostrophic balance: away from the ground, the pressure-gradient force is balanced by the Coriolis force, so the wind blows along the isobars, faster where they are closer. It weakens toward the equator and breaks down near terrain, which is exactly what happens in the channel.

Channel analysis: pressure gradient across the channel (stations)

What it shows

The pressure drop measured across the channel by shore stations, over the last 48 h, with RRFS's forecast for the next 48 h.

Data and timing

PHOG (Kahului), 023HI (Hamoa, near Hāna), 075HI (Iole, near Upolu Point), Kawaihae harbour (NOAA tide gauge), PHKO (Kona) and PHNY (Lānaʻi): NWS, Hawaiʻi Mesonet and NOAA CO-OPS, hourly.

How it's made
  • Each hour, a plane is fitted through the stations' sea-level pressures (at least four needed).
  • Self-calibration: each station's average misfit over the last five days is treated as a fixed offset (sensor height, reduction to sea level, calibration) and removed, then the plane is fitted again.
  • Drop toward the exit: how fast pressure falls along the axis toward 246°, per 100 nm.
  • Channel-wind potential: the speed air would reach running down that drop over the ~49 nm from entrance to exit, √(2Δp/ρ) (Bernoulli, ρ = 1.2 kg/m³).
  • RRFS's forecast uses the same fit through its sea-level pressure at the stations (without the observed offsets).
Assumptions and limits. The potential is an upper bound: friction and mixing keep the real wind well below it, but it rises and falls with the forcing. No geostrophic wind is given: this close to the tropics and the islands the local gradient is far from geostrophic balance (it would imply 100+ kt winds that never happen).
Learn. This is the gap-flow push itself: higher pressure where the trades pile up against Haleakalā and Kohala, lower in the lee. It is steeper than the GFS isobars because GFS's grid cannot resolve the islands' own pressure pattern.

Channel analysis: pressure gradient across the islands (buoys) and the inflow

What it shows

The pressure difference between buoys 51000 (northeast) and 51002 (southwest), ~450 nm apart, and the wind at each: the trade flow going in and coming out, and how well it lines up with the channel.

Data and timing

NDBC, hourly, last 24 h.

How it's made

Gradient = difference ÷ distance; geostrophic wind from it (air density 1.22 kg/m³, Coriolis at the buoys' mean latitude). Alignment = the acute angle between the buoy wind and the channel axis (0° = straight down it; within 15° is well lined up).

Assumptions and limits. It assumes the whole difference lies along the line between the buoys, so it gives the size of the push, not its direction.

Channel analysis: the trade-wind inversion (Hilo soundings)

What it shows

The inversion in the last four Hilo weather-balloon launches: its base and top (m and ft), how sharply it warms and dries, a strength index, the 925–850 hPa wind, and Skew-T diagrams.

Data and timing

Hilo (91285) radiosonde, launched at 00Z and 12Z (2 pm and 2 am HST). Newest launches from the University of Wyoming; older ones from the Iowa Environmental Mesonet archive. Kept once fetched.

How it's made
  • Search 950–600 hPa (below ~4 km) for a layer that warms and dries upward: net warming ≥0.8 °C, potential temperature rising ≥0.7 K, relative humidity falling ≥8%, 80–1,200 m thick. Among candidates, the one with the biggest drying wins.
  • Strength index (0–10) = 10·tanh(x/d), with x = (Δθ/2.5 K)·(ΔT/2.4 °C)·(ΔRH/45%) and d = thickness/300 m + 0.4: it favours sharp, dry layers of moderate thickness.
  • 925–850 hPa wind: the 75th percentile of the balloon's wind in that layer (the top of the trade-wind layer).
  • Risk: High = strength ≥6 and wind ≥30 kt; Medium for combinations of strength ≥3 with wind ≥23–30 kt; otherwise Low.
  • The Skew-T is drawn from the same data: temperature red, dew point green, the inversion shaded.
How to read it

Supportive lids usually have a base near 1.5–2.5 km, are 100–600 m thick, warm by 2 K or more and dry by 15–30% or more.

Assumptions and limits. Hilo is one point, about 60 nm from the channel on the windward side, and a launch is up to 12 h old. The strength index is this briefing's own scale.
Learn. The trade-wind inversion forms where air sinking in the Pacific High (and warming as it sinks) meets the cool, moist air of the trade-wind layer. On a Skew-T, it shows as the temperature line bending to the right (warming with height) while the dew point falls away (drying). Potential temperature (θ) rising sharply with height means a stable layer that air cannot easily cross, which is what confines the trades and squeezes them through the gap.

Model wind maps (models.html) and REFS maps (refs.html)

What it shows

Each model's wind over Maui to the Big Island, hour by hour, side by side in 1, 2 or 4 panes. The REFS page shows the ensemble mean, spread and chances of 20–40 kt.

How it's made
  • Colour is wind speed on each model's own grid, every 2.5 km cell as it is, with no smoothing, so lee eddies, wake vortices and the gap jet keep their real size. Grey streamlines show direction only. Magenta lines are sea-level pressure every 1 hPa (RRFS only, over water: pressure under Mauna Kea is an extrapolation).
  • Panes are matched on valid time, so runs from different cycles line up. Maps are hourly to 48 h into each run, then 3-hourly (REFS hourly to 60 h); at an hour a product lacks, the pane shows its nearest frame and says so.
  • Blink flips the first pane between the first two choices to show where they differ. Move over or tap a map to read the value there. Arrow keys step through the hours.
  • The UH WRF 2 km nest covers only north and west of the red dashes; outside it, use UH WRF 6 km.
How to read it

On the REFS maps, a high chance with a small spread is a confident windy forecast; a middling chance with a large spread is a real maybe.

Assumptions and limits. The colour palette is banded, not a smooth ramp (see conventions).

Verification (verify.html)

What it shows

How each forecast compared with what ASCAT measured, pass by pass and over the whole archive, most useful first: a plain-language summary per source and a fair head-to-head; lately (any window from 1 to 30 days); what a forecast has meant (“when it said 15–20 kt, ASCAT measured…”); how big the misses get; the conditions hardest to forecast; where in the channel the misses are; the Small Craft and gale thresholds; then lead time, month by month, every pass, and the methods.

Data and timing

ASCAT (MetOp-B and -C, KNMI 12.5 km coastal product, from NASA PO.DAAC); the NWS zone forecasts (every issue, from the Iowa Environmental Mesonet); NBM (NOAA's AWS archive); RRFS, REFS and UH WRF from the runs this briefing kept; the NWS grid, saved each build. Passes before the briefing ran were added from the public archives, including RRFS's official retrospective runs (July 2023, January–February 2024, May 2024).

How it's made
  • Each pass's good cells over the PHZ121 zone (cells with no wind, or flagged by KNMI for rain, a failed wind inversion, failed variational or KNMI quality control, beam noise or too few good backscatter values, are removed; land and ice flags only inform); a pass that saw at least 60% of the zone is scored.
  • Each model is averaged over every ASCAT cell's 12.5 km footprint (about 25 points of a 2.5 km grid), at the hour nearest the pass, so a pass that saw part of the zone compares like with like.
  • Bias = forecast minus ASCAT (red: ran high, blue: low); typical error = mean absolute error; lead is measured from each run's start (the zone forecast and NWS grid: from issue); “up to a day ahead”, used by every summary, = one run per source and pass, the one made nearest 12 h before (within 0–24 h), never an average of runs.
  • Lead and time of day: the UH WRF archive has one run a day (06Z) and the RRFS retrospective one (18Z), so for them a short lead is always one time of day and a long lead the other. The lead table is therefore split into morning and evening passes, and each source's typical lead and share of evening passes is shown with its headline. The same passes are also scored at 0–6 h and 18–30 h ahead, so a lead effect is not a sample effect (the zone forecast, for one, over-calls more in its last issues than a day out).
  • What is compared to what: the models against the mean of ASCAT's cells (both over the same cells); the zone forecast's range against their median, its top against their 90th percentile; the cell-by-cell scores cell against cell.
  • Head to head: sources are scored on different passes (RRFS mostly on its 2023–24 retrospective runs) and at different leads (the UH WRF archive keeps only each run's first hours), so each pair is also compared on only the passes and lead windows (0–6, 6–12, 12–24 h) they share, with how often each was the closer.
  • Reading a forecast: for each forecast speed band (and each zone-forecast range), what ASCAT then measured: median, 25th–75th and 90th percentiles over passes, and how often 5+ kt more than forecast. This turns the scores into a correction a reader can apply.
  • The zone forecast is checked against the middle (median) of what ASCAT saw: within its range, above or below; how far outside the range when it missed; its top number against ASCAT's 90th percentile of the zone's cells (P90, which a zone forecast has to cover); and its range width. The middle of its range is also scored like a model (“NWS zone text (middle)”), so it can be compared with the models everywhere on the page. “To 30 kt” with no lower number is read as 25–30, and a single number (“20 knots”) as 17.5–22.5.
  • How big the misses get: averages hide the misses that matter, so each source's whole spread of errors is shown as box plots (median, 25th–75th percentile box, 5th–95th percentile whiskers, the extreme passes as dots), a table of how often it under- or over-called by 5 and 10 kt and the error it stays within 90% of the time, histograms, and the archive's biggest misses with the conditions at the time. “Lately” shows each pass as a dot on a small ±15 kt strip, with the worst miss.
  • Cell by cell: how often cells were 5+ kt too low or high (a source can look fine on the zone average while a fifth of the zone is well off); the same scores in the channel's entrance, mid-channel and exit; the error at each cell, the correlation of the forecast pattern with ASCAT's, and how far the forecast's strongest fifth of the zone sits from ASCAT's; and error maps, filterable by season, month, wind and inversion.
  • Thresholds: 25 and 34 kt, caught / missed / false alarm. A zone-forecast headline counts only when its winds reach the threshold (advisories for seas alone are left out: ASCAT cannot see seas).
How to read it

Gusts are not verified here: ASCAT measures the sustained wind over each 12.5 km cell, never a gust. The models' gusts are kept with every pass, but there is no measurement of gusts over the channel's water to score them against (shore and land stations sit in their own terrain and don't represent it).

Assumptions and limits. See “What ASCAT can and can't tell us” at the top of the verification page. In short: ASCAT sees one moment around 09–10 and 20–22 HST, never the afternoon peak; many passes see only part of the channel; its own error is about 2–3 kt, so smaller differences are within its uncertainty. It misses cells within a few km of the coast; is spoiled by heavy rain; and tends to read a little low in very strong winds. RRFS, REFS and the NWS grid can be scored only from the runs the briefing kept, so their samples are shorter; archived UH WRF is mostly 1–3 h ahead, which flatters it against sources scored further out (the head-to-head allows for it). Small groups make noisy scores; every figure shows its count.
Learn. Verification is how forecasters learn a model's habits: a consistent bias (one model always low in the jet) is easy to correct for; a random error is not. The same data could one day train a bias-corrected blend for the channel.

Model versions. Upgrades can change a model's habits, so scores are also split by version: NBM went from v4.3 to v5.0 with the 12Z run of 5 May 2026 (the version is printed in NBM's station bulletins), and RRFS becomes operational on 14 Oct 2026. The month-by-month chart marks each change.

Glossary

AFD
Area Forecast Discussion: the NWS forecasters' written reasoning.
ASCAT
Advanced Scatterometer on the MetOp satellites: sea-surface wind from radar backscatter, 12.5 km cells.
Bias
Average of forecast minus observed: positive means the forecast runs high.
Correlation (pattern match)
How well two patterns line up: 1 = the stronger and weaker places match exactly, 0 = unrelated.
Ensemble
Many runs of a model with small differences, to show the range of possible outcomes.
Geostrophic wind
The wind that balances the pressure gradient against the Earth's rotation; it ignores friction and terrain.
GFS
NOAA's Global Forecast System, a worldwide model (0.25°, about 25 km).
Gust
A brief peak above the sustained (average) wind.
HST / Z
Hawaiʻi Standard Time (UTC−10) / UTC.
Inversion
A layer where temperature rises with height: a lid that air cannot easily cross.
Lead time
How long before the valid time a forecast was made.
MAE (typical error)
Mean absolute error: the average size of the miss, whichever way. Misses of +3 and −1 kt give a bias of +1 kt but an MAE of 2 kt.
NBM
National Blend of Models: NOAA's calibrated blend of many models.
Nm, kt
Nautical mile (1.852 km); knot (1 nm per hour).
POD / FAR
Probability of detection (share of events caught) / false-alarm ratio (share of warnings with no event).
REFS
The Rapid Refresh Ensemble Forecast System: the RRFS ensemble's products.
RMSE
Root-mean-square error: like MAE but weighting big misses more.
RRFS
Rapid Refresh Forecast System: NOAA's 2.5 km convection-allowing model, operational from 14 Oct 2026.
Skew-T
A chart of a sounding: temperature and dew point against pressure, with the temperature axis slanted.
Sounding
A weather balloon's profile of temperature, humidity and wind with height.
Spread
The ensemble's standard deviation: a measure of uncertainty.
Throat
The narrowest part of the channel, Upolu Point to the Kaupō–Kīpahulu coast.
UH WRF
The University of Hawaiʻi's run of the WRF model for the islands.
θ (theta)
Potential temperature: the temperature air would have brought down to 1000 hPa; rising θ with height means stable air.
