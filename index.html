About this report

This automated marine weather briefing is generated using data from the following official sources:

  • NWS Honolulu (HFO) coastal/offshore marine forecasts and AFD (via tgftp.nws.noaa.gov).
  • NOAA Ocean Prediction Center (OPC) Pacific surface, 500 mb, and wind/wave analyses.
  • NDBC buoys (e.g., 51000 and 51002) for pressure and wind observations.
  • NOAA GFS 0.25° mean sea-level pressure (PRMSL) fields for synoptic and channel isobar analysis (downloaded through the NOMADS grib filter, 0.25° with a 1° fallback).
  • NOAA/NESDIS STAR GOES‑18 Tropical West and HI sector geocolor imagery.
  • ASCAT Coastal UHR scatterometer winds (12.5 km) for coastal wind detail.
  • Hilo (91285) upper‑air soundings from the University of Wyoming (newest launches) and the Iowa Environmental Mesonet (older ones) for trade‑wind inversion detection; Skew‑T diagrams drawn from them.
  • Model wind comparison: NOAA RRFS (deterministic runs and ensemble members) and REFS (NOMADS), the National Blend of Models (AWS), UH WRF (PacIOOS THREDDS), and the NWS Honolulu gridded forecast (api.weather.gov).
  • Forecast verification (verify.html): ASCAT-B and -C 12.5 km coastal wind files (EUMETSAT OSI SAF / KNMI, via NASA PO.DAAC, found through NASA CMR) as the measurement; scored against them, the NWS zone forecasts (every issue, from the Iowa Environmental Mesonet archive), NBM (NOAA's AWS archive), RRFS, REFS and UH WRF runs kept by this briefing, the NOAA RRFS v1 retrospective runs (2023–24), the UH WRF archive at PacIOOS, and the NWS gridded forecast; the Hilo sounding before each pass for the inversion.

These sources are combined to provide a practical overview for the Alenuihāhā Channel, balancing large‑scale context (Pacific High, PRMSL) with local indicators (buoys, ASCAT, TWI). Time stamps are shown in both HST and UTC where applicable.

All data is pulled directly from official sources at the time of report generation. Click on any active link to view the most current data available.

Synopsis

A plume of shallow moisture lingering in a light to moderate trade wind flow will maintain increased cloud cover through morning, while wet conditions persist over mainly windward Big Island. An approaching disturbance aloft may trigger a few heavy showers this afternoon and tonight, mainly over Kauai and Oahu. The tail end of a front will move down the island chain late Sunday through Monday, bringing breezy northeasterly trades and drier air in its wake. Breezy easterly trades will bring periods of windward and mauka showers through the rest of the week.

Discussion

Stubborn shallow moisture continues to produce enhanced cloud cover over the island chain with somewhat wet conditions lingering on windward Big Island this morning. The surface trough that affected the state during the past few days is now over 50 miles southeast of Big Island and drifting away as light to moderate trade winds remain in place over the state. Deeper moisture pooled along this feature will continue to produce somewhat wet conditions across windward Big Island this morning, with a decrease in shower activity expected this afternoon as moisture decreases. A lingering band of shallow moisture continues to stretch across the remainder of the state and extends to an area of thunderstorms 100 to 500 miles west of Kauai. Despite high humidity, shower activity is expected to be limited from Maui to Kauai this morning, but as the upper level trough generating the thunderstorms to the west edges closer to the islands this afternoon, a few heavy showers could develop on Kauai and possibly Oahu.

Light trade winds will produce mainly windward rainfall from Big Island to Maui County tonight into early Sunday morning, while the above mentioned upper level trough passes just north of the state and possibly generates more shower activity near Kauai and Oahu. The ECMWF is suggesting higher chances of rainfall over the western end of the island chain, while the GFS is keeping the heavier showers away from the islands. As a result, confidence is lower than usual.

A shallow front will break apart as it moves down the island chain late Sunday and Sunday night. While this feature will deliver some rainfall to windward and north facing terrain, the main impact will be the development of breezy and gusty northeasterly trade winds from west to east. Deep high pressure building behind the front will usher in a drier and more stable air mass, and with dew points potentially dropping into the lower 60s or may even the upper 50s, this will feel more like the first shot of winterlike conditions than just relief from the recent humidity.

A breezy and stable trade wind flow will continue through at least the end of the work week as high pressure holds north of the state. Dew points will remain quite low on Tuesday, then gradually rebound closer to normal. Expect rainfall to be largely focused across windward slopes, with passing light rainfall leeward.

Heading into next weekend, focus returns to the tropics again. While the details are out of agreement, the GFS and ECMWF are showing possible tropical cyclone development far southeast of the state late in the week with some chance of a tropical cyclone being near or south the islands next weekend. The National Hurricane Center continues to monitor this area, maintaining a medium (40 percent) chance of development over the next 7 days.

Aviation

A trough to the west and ridge to the east will maintain easterly trade winds through the morning, with significant differences in model solutions later by mid morning. Light showers and clouds will focus over primarily windward and mountain areas this morning. The exception will be windward Big Island, where increased moisture will linger through the forecast period, keeping lower ceilings and reduced visibility in place. This afternoon and evening's conditions will depend on the progression of the trough to the west. Half of our model solutions depict this feature bringing deeper moisture over Kauai and moving down to Oahu this afternoon through tonight, along with veering winds to the southeast. This solution would mean more showers and low ceilings/reduced visibility over Oahu and Kauai tonight. The other half of our models show drier conditions with light easterly winds. There is also the chance for some pop up afternoon showers today as low level moisture associated with the trough lingers.

AIRMET Sierra for mountain obscuration remains in effect abv 020 for windward areas of the Big Island, Maui, and Molokai. This could remain through the early morning before improving late morning.

Marine

High pressure to the north will bring moderate to fresh trades to the region today. Latest (Sat 01:00 HST): pressure falls 2.8 hPa per 100 nm toward the channel exit (3-hour median), worth up to 28 kt of channel wind; it falls fastest toward 191°, 55° off the exit direction (5 stations, fit error 0.11 hPa).

Why this differs from the isobars above. The GFS field in Local Isobar Field has pressure falling 1.3 hPa per 100 nm toward 205°; the stations measure 2.2 toward 191°: in about the same direction and about as steep, and both are right at their own scale. GFS fits a 180 nm box on a 0.25° grid that cannot resolve the islands; the stations sit on either side of the islands' own pressure pattern, higher on the windward side where the trades pile up against Haleakalā and Kohala and lower in the lee. That local difference is what drives the channel jet, so it is the one to watch here. RRFS, at 2.5 km, puts 2.9 hPa per 100 nm toward 200° at the same stations, about 1.3 times what they measure: a model that resolves the islands sees the steep local gradient too.

How it works

The push through the channel measured at its own scale. Each hour a flat pressure surface is fitted through the stations around the channel that report sea-level pressure (Kahului airport (PHOG), Hamoa (near Hana) (023HI), Iole (near Upolu Pt) (075HI), Kawaihae harbor (1617433), Kona airport (PHKO), Lanai airport (PHNY)), after removing each station's constant offset over the last five days. Pressure drop toward the exit is how fast pressure falls along the channel axis toward the exit (066°/246°): the forcing that accelerates air through the channel. Channel-wind potential is the speed air would reach running down that drop from the entrance to the exit (~49 nm), √(2Δp/ρ): an upper bound, since friction and mixing hold the real wind below it, but it rises and falls with the forcing. The dashed red line is the same calculation from RRFS's forecast pressure at the same stations, so you can see whether the observed gradient is running ahead of the model or behind it. No geostrophic wind is given: this close to the tropics and the islands the local gradient is far from geostrophic balance.

Trade-Wind Inversion (Hilo soundings)

Latest launch: no clear trade-wind inversion below 4 km, so nothing is capping the flow and the channel wind can mix upward.
How it works

The inversion is a lid on the trade winds: a strong, low one squeezes the flow through the channel and speeds it up; a weak or missing one lets it mix upward. Each twice-daily balloon from Hilo (91285) is searched for a layer below ~4 km that warms and dries upward. Warming Δθ is the jump in potential temperature across it (stability), Drying ΔRH the drop in relative humidity, and the strength index (0–10) combines them, favouring sharp, dry layers of moderate thickness (weak below 3, moderate 3–6, strong 6 or more). Supportive lids usually have a base near 1.5–2.5 km, 100–600 m thick, Δθ of 2 K or more and ΔRH of 15–30% or more. Check the Skew-T diagrams for the full profile.
