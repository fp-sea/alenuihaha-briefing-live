About this report

This automated marine weather briefing is generated using data from the following official sources:

  • NWS Honolulu (HFO) coastal/offshore marine forecasts and AFD (via tgftp.nws.noaa.gov).
  • NOAA Ocean Prediction Center (OPC) Pacific surface, 500 mb, and wind/wave analyses.
  • NDBC buoys (e.g., 51000 and 51002) for pressure and wind observations.
  • NOAA GFS 0.25° mean sea-level pressure (PRMSL) fields for synoptic and channel isobar analysis (downloaded through the NOMADS grib filter, 0.25° with a 1° fallback).
  • NOAA/NESDIS STAR GOES‑18 Tropical West and HI sector geocolor imagery.
  • ASCAT Coastal UHR scatterometer winds (12.5 km) for coastal wind detail.
  • Hilo (91285) upper‑air soundings from the University of Wyoming (newest launches) and the Iowa Environmental Mesonet (older ones) for trade‑wind inversion detection; Skew‑T diagrams drawn from them.
  • Model wind comparison: NOAA RRFS (deterministic runs and ensemble members) and REFS (NOMADS), the National Blend of Models (AWS), UH WRF (PacIOOS THREDDS), and the NWS Honolulu gridded forecast (api.weather.gov).
  • Forecast verification (verify.html): ASCAT-B and -C 12.5 km coastal wind files (EUMETSAT OSI SAF / KNMI, via NASA PO.DAAC, found through NASA CMR) as the measurement; scored against them, the NWS zone forecasts (every issue, from the Iowa Environmental Mesonet archive), NBM (NOAA's AWS archive), RRFS, REFS and UH WRF runs kept by this briefing, the NOAA RRFS v1 retrospective runs (2023–24), the UH WRF archive at PacIOOS, and the NWS gridded forecast; the Hilo sounding before each pass for the inversion.

These sources are combined to provide a practical overview for the Alenuihāhā Channel, balancing large‑scale context (Pacific High, PRMSL) with local indicators (buoys, ASCAT, TWI). Time stamps are shown in both HST and UTC where applicable.

Honolulu Area Forecast Discussion

Issued Sat 07 Nov 3:39 AM HST (13:39Z), 3 h agoCurrentOfficial text
Synopsis

A high pressure ridge to the north of the state will remain locked in place for the foreseeable future keeping Tropical Storm Pena moving in a westerly direction, passing well south of the Hawaiian Islands. Expect a transition to breezy trade winds today with drier trends through the weekend, lasting into the first half of next week. The next best chance for increasing statewide shower activity arrives from next week Thursday to Friday as an unsettled cloud band associated with a dissipated cold front moves in from the north.

Discussion

The satellite imagery this morning shows more stable stratocumulus clouds east of the Hawaiian Islands riding in on the trade winds. These low level cloud features are more stable indicating stronger subsidence aloft and lower trade wind temperature inversion heights. This upstream stability validates our drying trends though Sunday as the upper low that was anchored west of the state dissipates and the deep tropical moisture surge associated with the passing easterly wave wanes.

Tropical Storm Pena remains roughly 525 miles south-southeast of South Point on the Big Island of Hawaii. Pena will continue on a east to west track under the steering influence of the lingering high pressure ridge north of the state keeping the tropical storm impacts well south of the island chain. Easterly winds may approach wind advisory thresholds in some terrain favored areas. However, at this time we are not expecting to issue wind advisories as Tropical Storm Pena passes south of the state.

Large scale atmospheric downward vertical motions (subsidence) over the next several days will cause the trade wind temperature inversion heights to fall, lowering cloud heights, and limiting shower activity from later today through Wednesday. Temperature inversion heights will range from 6,000 to 7,000 feet elevation during this time period, high enough for brief passing showers mainly along windward mountain ranges, favoring the overnight to early morning hours. Rainfall amounts over Maui and the Big Island will trend lower as compared to the past several days.

The next best chance for increasing statewide shower activity arrives from next week Thursday to Friday as an unsettled cloud band associated with a dissipated cold front moves in from the north. Expect northern and eastern slopes of all islands to see additional beneficial rainfall as breezy easterly trade winds lift these unsettled clouds up island mountain slopes.

Aviation

Moderate to locally breezy trade winds will persist through the period. Pockets of higher moisture riding in on the trades early this morning will guarantee occasional lower decks falling to low VFR or MVFR flight categories along with more frequent windward showers especially at Hilo (PHTO). Better organized rain clusters passing over windward terminals this morning will produce more frequent periods of MVFR in both lowered ceiling and visibility. Conditions will begin to slowly improve this afternoon as drier air moves in from the east.

AIRMET Sierra is in effect for mountain obscuration across windward Big Island, Maui, Molokai and Oahu. Sierra will likely will allowed to expire by noon with lifting decks to VFR levels.

AIRMET Tango remains in effect for moderate turbulence within the lee of higher terrain.

Marine

Fresh to strong trade winds will prevail through through the remainder of this weekend as high pressure dominates north of the state and Tropical Storm Pena moves westward, several hundred miles south of the state. The Small Craft Advisory (SCA) has been extended through Sunday night for most Hawaiian Waters due to a combination of winds and elevated seas. The Big Island windward waters were dropped due to wind speed guidence falling just below SCA criteria. Latest (Sat 04:00 HST): pressure falls 4.0 hPa per 100 nm toward the channel exit (3-hour median), worth up to 33 kt of channel wind; it falls fastest toward 204°, 42° off the exit direction (4 stations, fit error 0.21 hPa).

Why this is steeper than the isobars above. The GFS field in Local Isobar Field has pressure falling 2.1 hPa per 100 nm toward 203°; the stations measure 8.6 toward 204°: in about the same direction and about 4 times steeper, and both are right at their own scale. GFS fits a 180 nm box on a 0.25° grid that cannot resolve the islands; the stations sit on either side of the islands' own pressure pattern, higher on the windward side where the trades pile up against Haleakalā and Kohala and lower in the lee. That local difference is what drives the channel jet, so it is the one to watch here. How it works

The push through the channel measured at its own scale. Each hour a flat pressure surface is fitted through the stations around the channel that report sea-level pressure (Kahului airport (PHOG), Hamoa (near Hana) (023HI), Iole (near Upolu Pt) (075HI), Kawaihae harbor (1617433), Kona airport (PHKO), Lanai airport (PHNY)), after removing each station's constant offset over the last five days. Pressure drop toward the exit is how fast pressure falls along the channel axis toward the exit (066°/246°): the forcing that accelerates air through the channel. Channel-wind potential is the speed air would reach running down that drop from the entrance to the exit (~49 nm), √(2Δp/ρ): an upper bound, since friction and mixing hold the real wind below it, but it rises and falls with the forcing. The dashed red line is the same calculation from RRFS's forecast pressure at the same stations, so you can see whether the observed gradient is running ahead of the model or behind it. No geostrophic wind is given: this close to the tropics and the islands the local gradient is far from geostrophic balance.

Trade-Wind Inversion (Hilo soundings)

Latest launch: an inversion from 3,136 to 3,269 m, warming 3.7 K and drying 70% across it: strength 9.8 of 10, strong.
How it works

The inversion is a lid on the trade winds: a strong, low one squeezes the flow through the channel and speeds it up; a weak or missing one lets it mix upward. Each twice-daily balloon from Hilo (91285) is searched for a layer below ~4 km that warms and dries upward. Warming Δθ is the jump in potential temperature across it (stability), Drying ΔRH the drop in relative humidity, and the strength index (0–10) combines them, favouring sharp, dry layers of moderate thickness (weak below 3, moderate 3–6, strong 6 or more). Supportive lids usually have a base near 1.5–2.5 km, 100–600 m thick, Δθ of 2 K or more and ΔRH of 15–30% or more. Check the Skew-T diagrams for the full profile.
