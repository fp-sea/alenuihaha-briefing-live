About this report

This automated marine weather briefing is generated using data from the following official sources:

  • NWS Honolulu (HFO) coastal/offshore marine forecasts and AFD (via tgftp.nws.noaa.gov).
  • NOAA Ocean Prediction Center (OPC) Pacific surface, 500 mb, and wind/wave analyses.
  • NDBC buoys (e.g., 51000 and 51002) for pressure and wind observations.
  • NOAA GFS 0.25° mean sea-level pressure (PRMSL) fields for synoptic and channel isobar analysis (downloaded through the NOMADS grib filter, 0.25° with a 1° fallback).
  • NOAA/NESDIS STAR GOES‑18 Tropical West and HI sector geocolor imagery.
  • ASCAT Coastal UHR scatterometer winds (12.5 km) for coastal wind detail.
  • Hilo (91285) upper‑air soundings from the University of Wyoming (newest launches) and the Iowa Environmental Mesonet (older ones) for trade‑wind inversion detection; Skew‑T diagrams drawn from them.
  • Model wind comparison: NOAA RRFS (deterministic runs and ensemble members) and REFS (NOMADS), the National Blend of Models (AWS), UH WRF (PacIOOS THREDDS), and the NWS Honolulu gridded forecast (api.weather.gov).

These sources are combined to provide a practical overview for the Alenuihāhā Channel, balancing large‑scale context (Pacific High, PRMSL) with local indicators (buoys, ASCAT, TWI). Time stamps are shown in both HST and UTC where applicable.

Synopsis

A drier air mass with lower humidity, sunny skies and light to moderate winds expected through Sunday. Isolated showers possible windward and mauka spots. Winds should decrease early next week allowing land and sea breezes to develop.

Discussion

Looking at satellite and radar imagery this morning, isolated showers are ongoing along windward and mauka spots across the state. High pressure is expected to build back over the state dropping humidity levels and ushering in sunny skies through Sunday.

Early next week, trades will weaken in response to an approaching surface trough and then will weaken further heading into midweek as broad troughing develops northwest of the state. In this lighter flow, land and sea breezes will develop with clearing expected overnight and clouds and a few showers over leeward and interior areas each afternoon.

Aviation

The high pressure ridge will build back over the Hawaii region with drier easterly trade wind weather returning just in time for the weekend. Lower humidity levels and mostly sunny skies will prevail through Sunday.

No AIRMETs in effect.

Marine

Gentle to fresh east to northeast trade winds fill back in today as Hurricane Nolo accelerates westward away from the Hawaiian Islands and high pressure strengthens gradually to the north. A Small Craft Advisory has been issued, in effect beginning tonight, when locally strong trades are forecast to develop for the typically windy waters and channels. Locally strong trades may persist into early next week before high pressure to the north weakens again and winds start to diminish. By the middle of next week, troughing developing northwest of the islands may cause winds to veer out of the southeast once again.

A small background south swell will surf mostly steady along south facing shores through the weekend. A new small, long-period south swell, originating from a large fetch of gale force winds southeast of New Zealand earlier this week, is forecast to arrive beginning Sunday and peak Monday through Wednesday. This will push surf to slightly above seasonal averages for the first half of next week.

A small bump in surf along north-facing shores will decrease today as a small, short period north swell fades. Another small bump in north shore surf is possible early next week as a small, medium-period northwest swell arrives late Sunday and peaks on Monday. This will be followed by a slightly larger long-period northwest swell peaking on Wednesday, giving a good boost to north shore surf for mid-week. Surf along east facing shores will increase and become more choppy through tonight as easterly trades build back into the region.

Fire weather

Drier air is expected over the weekend as light to moderate east- northeasterly trades return. Winds are expected to decrease early to midweek. Isolated showers will favor windward and mauka areas overnight into the early morning hours. Fire weather conditions will remain below critical threshold this week for both wind and humidity levels. Temperature inversion heights are around 5000-7000ft.

REFS ensemble by area

REFS is NOAA's high-resolution ensemble: it combines the latest and 6 h older RRFS runs with the RRFS ensemble members, so it can say how likely a wind is rather than giving one answer. Run 12Z Sat 03 Oct (Sat 02:00 HST), hourly to 60 h. Top: ensemble-mean wind with a band of ± one spread; the spread is the ensemble's standard deviation, so roughly two members in three fall inside the band. A wide band means the members disagree. In the Area average view the dotted lines are the lowest and highest area-average wind among the 11 runs at each time (every 3 h). They cover every run rather than two in three, so they often sit outside the band: the gap between them is the full range of outcomes the runs give. REFS's mean wind speed runs above the individual RRFS winds (about 1 kt mid-channel, up to ~5 kt along the Kohala coast where directions vary), so read it as the upper side of typical rather than the middle. Bottom: the chance of the wind reaching a threshold (tap a threshold to hide its line). Drag the slider to read every value at one hour.

The two buttons ask the same runs two different questions; they are not two separate opinions. REFS is made from the RRFS ensemble members plus the deterministic RRFS, and the 11 runs here are those runs (the five members of the latest run and of the run 6 h before, plus the deterministic RRFS). Area average asks each run for its average wind over this area and counts how many reach 25 kt (small craft advisory), 30 kt or 34 kt (gale), every 3 h to 60 h. Within ~20 nm (REFS) is NOAA's own product: the chance of 20, 30, 35 or 40 kt anywhere within roughly 20 nm of a point, so an area beside the jet can show a high chance while its own average wind stays light. For deciding whether the water you will be on reaches a threshold, the area average is the more direct answer.

24-hour HST. Civil twilight (sun 6° down) is the light left after sunset, enough to see other boats and the harbour entrance: the practical limit for coming in. By nautical dusk (12° down) the horizon is gone. Twilight is short this close to the tropics, about 22 minutes, so dark comes quickly after sunset. Local apparent noon (LAN) is when the sun crosses the meridian: highest in the sky, the time of a noon sight and of the strongest UV. The channel is 6° west of the meridian HST keeps time by, so LAN comes about 24 minutes after clock noon on average: from about 12:07 in early November to about 12:38 in mid February, as the equation of time runs through the year. The altitude is the sun's height above the horizon then, and which way it bears (south for most of the year; north from late May to mid July, when the sun passes overhead).

How it works

Local Isobar Field over the Channel

Now: the pressure pattern over the channel implies a geostrophic wind of 15 kt from 107°, 41° off the channel axis (11 kt of it along the axis). Read it as how hard the pattern is pushing, not as the wind on the water.
How it works

A flat pressure surface is fitted to the GFS sea-level pressure over the ocean in a ~180 nm box around the channel (land excluded), for T−24h, now, T+24h and T+48h. Its slope gives the geostrophic wind: speed, direction, the angle to the channel axis (066°/246°) and the part along it. The box spans the whole channel, so there is one value per time; differences between the entrance, mid-channel and exit come from the high-resolution models. Geostrophic wind ignores friction and the terrain's squeeze, so the real channel wind can be well above or below it.

Pressure Gradient Across the Channel (Stations)

Latest (Sat 06:00 HST): pressure falls 0.2 hPa per 100 nm toward the channel exit (3-hour median), worth up to 8 kt of channel wind; it falls fastest toward 179°, 67° off the exit direction (6 stations, fit error 0.27 hPa).

Why this is steeper than the isobars above. The GFS field in Local Isobar Field has pressure falling 0.9 hPa per 100 nm toward 197°; the stations measure 2.1 toward 179°: in about the same direction and about 2 times steeper, and both are right at their own scale. GFS fits a 180 nm box on a 0.25° grid that cannot resolve the islands; the stations sit on either side of the islands' own pressure pattern, higher on the windward side where the trades pile up against Haleakalā and Kohala and lower in the lee. That local difference is what drives the channel jet, so it is the one to watch here. RRFS, at 2.5 km, puts 2.6 hPa per 100 nm toward 198° at the same stations, about 1.3 times what they measure: a model that resolves the islands sees the steep local gradient too.

How it works

The push through the channel measured at its own scale. Each hour a flat pressure surface is fitted through the stations around the channel that report sea-level pressure (Kahului airport (PHOG), Hamoa (near Hana) (023HI), Iole (near Upolu Pt) (075HI), Kawaihae harbor (1617433), Kona airport (PHKO), Lanai airport (PHNY)), after removing each station's constant offset over the last five days. Pressure drop toward the exit is how fast pressure falls along the channel axis toward the exit (066°/246°): the forcing that accelerates air through the channel. Channel-wind potential is the speed air would reach running down that drop from the entrance to the exit (~49 nm), √(2Δp/ρ): an upper bound, since friction and mixing hold the real wind below it, but it rises and falls with the forcing. The dashed red line is the same calculation from RRFS's forecast pressure at the same stations, so you can see whether the observed gradient is running ahead of the model or behind it. No geostrophic wind is given: this close to the tropics and the islands the local gradient is far from geostrophic balance.

Trade-Wind Inversion (Hilo soundings)

Latest launch: an inversion from 2,159 to 2,539 m, warming 5.2 K and drying 59% across it (strength 6.3 of 10).
How it works

The inversion is a lid on the trade winds: a strong, low one squeezes the flow through the channel and speeds it up; a weak or missing one lets it mix upward. Each twice-daily balloon from Hilo (91285) is searched for a layer below ~4 km that warms and dries upward. Warming Δθ is the jump in potential temperature across it (stability), Drying ΔRH the drop in relative humidity, and the strength index (0–10) combines them, favouring sharp, dry layers of moderate thickness. Supportive lids usually have a base near 1.5–2.5 km, 100–600 m thick, Δθ of 2 K or more and ΔRH of 15–30% or more. Check the Skew-T diagrams for the full profile.
