About this report

This automated marine weather briefing is generated using data from the following official sources:

  • NWS Honolulu (HFO) coastal/offshore marine forecasts and AFD (via tgftp.nws.noaa.gov).
  • NOAA Ocean Prediction Center (OPC) Pacific surface, 500 mb, and wind/wave analyses.
  • NDBC buoys (e.g., 51000 and 51002) for pressure and wind observations.
  • NOAA GFS 0.25° mean sea-level pressure (PRMSL) fields for synoptic and channel isobar analysis (downloaded through the NOMADS grib filter, 0.25° with a 1° fallback).
  • NOAA/NESDIS STAR GOES‑18 Tropical West and HI sector geocolor imagery.
  • ASCAT Coastal UHR scatterometer winds (12.5 km) for coastal wind detail.
  • Hilo (91285) upper‑air soundings from the University of Wyoming (newest launches) and the Iowa Environmental Mesonet (older ones) for trade‑wind inversion detection; Skew‑T diagrams drawn from them.
  • Model wind comparison: NOAA RRFS (deterministic runs and ensemble members) and REFS (NOMADS), the National Blend of Models (AWS), UH WRF (PacIOOS THREDDS), and the NWS Honolulu gridded forecast (api.weather.gov).
  • Forecast verification (verify.html): ASCAT-B and -C 12.5 km coastal wind files (EUMETSAT OSI SAF / KNMI, via NASA PO.DAAC, found through NASA CMR) as the measurement; scored against them, the NWS zone forecasts (every issue, from the Iowa Environmental Mesonet archive), NBM (NOAA's AWS archive), RRFS, REFS and UH WRF runs kept by this briefing, the NOAA RRFS v1 retrospective runs (2023–24), the UH WRF archive at PacIOOS, and the NWS gridded forecast; the Hilo sounding before each pass for the inversion.

These sources are combined to provide a practical overview for the Alenuihāhā Channel, balancing large‑scale context (Pacific High, PRMSL) with local indicators (buoys, ASCAT, TWI). Time stamps are shown in both HST and UTC where applicable.

All data is pulled directly from official sources at the time of report generation. Click on any active link to view the most current data available.

Passing showers are possible over windward and mauka areas during this time period, mainly during the overnight to early morning hours. The next best chance for statewide rainfall should arrive Tuesday and Wednesday as increased low level moisture arrives with an old cold front drifting into the region.

Discussion

Radar indicates scattered light showers across windward and mauka areas, and the open waters early this morning. Some continued uptick in coverage is expected this morning, but showers will weaken outside of windward areas this afternoon.

Mid level ridging will remain centered overhead through the middle of next week. Surface high pressure northwest of the islands today will slowly slide east to our northeast Sunday night. An upper level low will approach from the northwest Tuesday. In addition, a band of low level moisture associated with an old frontal boundary will move across the state from the east and northeast. These features will lead to an increased chance for showers midweek. Maintained very high PoPs for windward and mauka locations, but even leeward areas may see a few showers at times.

The upper low will dissipate as it finally reaches Hawaii around Thursday. Weak, broad ridging may build somewhat to our west late week and next weekend. This flow pattern may allow for more old fronts to pass through the state, but the timing of these is of low confidence at this range.

Aviation

Trade winds will continue with brief periods of passing showers over windward and mountain areas favoring the overnight to early morning hours. Improving weather trends during the daylight hours.

The push through the channel measured at its own scale. Trade-Wind Inversion (Hilo soundings)

Latest launch: an inversion from 1,900 to 1,996 m, warming 6.0 K and drying 72% across it: strength 10.0 of 10, strong.
How it works

The inversion is a lid on the trade winds: a strong, low one squeezes the flow through the channel and speeds it up; a weak or missing one lets it mix upward. Each twice-daily balloon from Hilo (91285) is searched for a layer below ~4 km that warms and dries upward. Warming Δθ is the jump in potential temperature across it (stability), Drying ΔRH the drop in relative humidity, and the strength index (0–10) combines them, favouring sharp, dry layers of moderate thickness (weak below 3, moderate 3–6, strong 6 or more).
