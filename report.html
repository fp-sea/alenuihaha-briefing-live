About this report

This automated marine weather briefing is generated using data from the following official sources:

  • NWS Honolulu (HFO) coastal/offshore marine forecasts and AFD (via tgftp.nws.noaa.gov).
  • NOAA Ocean Prediction Center (OPC) Pacific surface, 500 mb, and wind/wave analyses.
  • NDBC buoys (e.g., 51000 and 51002) for pressure and wind observations.
  • NOAA GFS 0.25° mean sea-level pressure (PRMSL) fields for synoptic and channel isobar analysis (downloaded through the NOMADS grib filter, 0.25° with a 1° fallback).
  • NOAA/NESDIS STAR GOES‑18 Tropical West and HI sector geocolor imagery.
  • ASCAT Coastal UHR scatterometer winds (12.5 km) for coastal wind detail.
  • Hilo (91285) upper‑air soundings from the University of Wyoming (newest launches) and the Iowa Environmental Mesonet (older ones) for trade‑wind inversion detection; Skew‑T diagrams drawn from them.
  • Model wind comparison: NOAA RRFS (deterministic runs and ensemble members) and REFS (NOMADS), the National Blend of Models (AWS), UH WRF (PacIOOS THREDDS), and the NWS Honolulu gridded forecast (api.weather.gov).
  • Forecast verification (verify.html): ASCAT-B and -C 12.5 km coastal wind files (EUMETSAT OSI SAF / KNMI, via NASA PO.DAAC, found through NASA CMR) as the measurement; scored against them, the NWS zone forecasts (every issue, from the Iowa Environmental Mesonet archive), NBM (NOAA's AWS archive), RRFS, REFS and UH WRF runs kept by this briefing, the NOAA RRFS v1 retrospective runs (2023–24), the UH WRF archive at PacIOOS, and the NWS gridded forecast; the Hilo sounding before each pass for the inversion.

These sources are combined to provide a practical overview for the Alenuihāhā Channel, balancing large‑scale context (Pacific High, PRMSL) with local indicators (buoys, ASCAT, TWI). Time stamps are shown in both HST and UTC where applicable.

Synopsis

Light to moderate trade winds are expected through the weekend. Brief passing showers will drift into the islands on the northeasterly trade winds, favoring windward and mountain areas during the overnight to early morning hours. A more significant change to our weather pattern continues to evolve for next week. Deep tropical moisture moving across the state will bring the potential for heavy rain and thunderstorms to most islands starting around Tuesday and persisting through at least Thursday.

Discussion

A surface trough just east of the state is visible on satellite imagery this afternoon, with peripheral clouds and showers tracking into east-facing slopes of the Big Island. This feature will maintain enhanced cloud cover and isolated to scattered showers across windward Big Island and Maui through tonight, before drifting eastward and pulling its deeper moisture away from the state.

Why this differs from the isobars above. The GFS field in Local Isobar Field has pressure falling 0.9 hPa per 100 nm toward 175°; the stations measure 4.5 toward 207°: 32° apart in direction and about 5 times steeper, and both are right at their own scale. GFS fits a 180 nm box on a 0.25° grid that cannot resolve the islands; the stations sit on either side of the islands' own pressure pattern, higher on the windward side where the trades pile up against Haleakalā and Kohala and lower in the lee. How it works

The push through the channel measured at its own scale. Pressure drop toward the exit is how fast pressure falls along the channel axis toward the exit (066°/246°): the forcing that accelerates air through the channel. Channel-wind potential is the speed air would reach running down that drop from the entrance to the exit (~49 nm), √(2Δp/ρ): an upper bound, since friction and mixing hold the real wind below it, but it rises and falls with the forcing. The dashed red line is the same calculation from RRFS's forecast pressure at the same stations, so you can see whether the observed gradient is running ahead of the model or behind it. How it works

Buoy 51000 (23.5°N, 153.8°W) lies northeast of the islands and buoy 51002 (17.1°N, 157.8°W) southwest of them, about 450 nm apart. Their pressure difference is the large-scale push across the Hawaiian chain; their winds are the trade flow going in and coming out. The angle "off the channel axis" is between each buoy's wind and the axis (066°/246°); winds lined up with the channel funnel through it more readily.

Trade-Wind Inversion (Hilo soundings)

Latest launch: no clear trade-wind inversion below 4 km, so nothing is capping the flow and the channel wind can mix upward.
How it works

The inversion is a lid on the trade winds: a strong, low one squeezes the flow through the channel and speeds it up; a weak or missing one lets it mix upward. Each twice-daily balloon from Hilo (91285) is searched for a layer below ~4 km that warms and dries upward. Warming Δθ is the jump in potential temperature across it (stability), Drying ΔRH the drop in relative humidity, and the strength index (0–10) combines them, favouring sharp, dry layers of moderate thickness (weak below 3, moderate 3–6, strong 6 or more). Supportive lids usually have a base near 1.5–2.5 km, 100–600 m thick, Δθ of 2 K or more and ΔRH of 15–30% or more. Check the Skew-T diagrams for the full profile.
